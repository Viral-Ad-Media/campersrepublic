Plenty of people who love camping still dread the night. The good news is that most poor tent sleep has a handful of fixable causes: the ground is cold, the surface is uneven, you're too hot or too cold, or the sounds keep you on edge. Work through these tips and you will notice the difference on your next trip.

## 1. Insulate from the ground first

The ground pulls heat out of your body much faster than the air does. A sleeping bag gets compressed underneath you, so it does very little there. Your pad does that job.

Sleeping pads are rated by **R-value**, which measures insulation. As a rough guide:

| Conditions | Suggested R-value |
|---|---|
| Warm summer nights | 2 to 3 |
| Three-season camping | 3 to 5 |
| Near or below freezing | 5 and up |

You can stack two pads and add their R-values together. A cheap closed-cell foam pad under an inflatable one is a classic, budget-friendly cold-weather setup.

## 2. Choose your site carefully

Lie down on the spot before you pitch. Look for flat ground without roots or rocks, and avoid low points where cold air and rainwater collect. If there's a slight slope, sleep with your head uphill.

## 3. Upgrade your pillow

Rolling up a jacket works, but a proper camp pillow or a regular pillow from home is one of the cheapest comfort upgrades there is. Neck pain is a common reason people say they "can't sleep camping".

## 4. Dress for bed, not for the day

Change into dry clothes before bed. The clothes you wore all day hold sweat, and damp fabric chills you. Keep a set of base layers, a warm hat and thick socks just for sleeping.

## 5. Eat a snack and warm up before bed

Your body produces heat by digesting food. A snack with some fat and protein before bed helps keep you warm. A few minutes of brisk walking or some sit-ups gets you into your bag already warm, and the bag only traps heat; it doesn't create it.

## 6. Don't go to bed needing the toilet

It sounds obvious, but holding it in keeps you awake and wastes body heat. Go right before bed. On cold trips, many campers keep a clearly labeled pee bottle in the tent.

## 7. Manage condensation

Your breath puts a surprising amount of moisture into a tent overnight. When it hits the cold tent walls it condenses and drips. To reduce it:

- Open the vents, even when it's cold
- Leave a door partly unzipped if the weather allows
- Don't cook or dry wet gear inside the tent
- Pitch under trees rather than in an open meadow if it's safe to do so

## 8. Block out light and sound

Campgrounds get early sunrises, late campfire conversations and plenty of wildlife noise. Foam earplugs and an eye mask take up almost no space and solve both problems.

## 9. Keep cool on hot nights

Heat can be just as bad as cold. Pitch where you'll get morning shade, remove the rainfly if there's no chance of rain, and use a sheet or a quilt instead of a zipped-up bag. A small battery fan pointed at your face can make a big difference.

## 10. Recreate your bedtime routine

Brushing your teeth, a few pages of a book, the same pre-sleep stretch: familiar routines signal to your brain that it's time to sleep, even in an unfamiliar place.

## 11. Give it a night

Many people sleep lightly on the first night anywhere new. It's a normal response and usually fades by the second night. If you camp often, it fades faster.

## A quick checklist for better tent sleep

- [ ] Pad with an R-value matched to the forecast
- [ ] Flat, well-drained site
- [ ] Real pillow
- [ ] Dry sleep clothes, hat and socks
- [ ] Snack and toilet stop before bed
- [ ] Vents open
- [ ] Earplugs and eye mask
