Living in an RV appeals to retirees, remote workers, young families and anyone who's dreamed of waking up somewhere new. It can be wonderful, but it isn't a permanent vacation. Here are seven upsides and seven downsides to weigh honestly.

## Types of RV at a glance

| Type | What it is | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Class A | Bus-style motorhome | Full-timers wanting maximum space |
| Class B (camper van) | Van conversion | Solo travelers and couples, easy parking |
| Class C | Motorhome with an over-cab bunk | Families |
| Travel trailer | Towed behind a truck or SUV | Flexible, leave it at camp and drive the tow vehicle |
| Fifth wheel | Large trailer hitched in a pickup bed | Long stays and spacious living |

## The pros

### 1. Freedom to move
If you don't like the neighbors, the weather or the view, you can move. You can follow good weather through the seasons or stay near family for a few months.

### 2. Potentially lower living costs
For some people, RV life costs less than a mortgage or rent. Monthly campground rates, volunteer camp hosting and free public land camping can keep costs down. That said, it depends heavily on your rig, fuel and travel pace.

### 3. Living with less
With limited space, you only keep what you use. Many RVers say this is freeing and cuts spending on things.

### 4. Time outdoors
National parks, beaches and forests become your backyard. Outdoor activities stop being something you schedule.

### 5. Community
Full-time RVers have a strong community of rallies, online groups and campground friendships that often last for years.

### 6. Your home travels with you
Same bed, same kitchen, same coffee mug wherever you go. No packing and unpacking suitcases.

### 7. A new kind of family time
Families who travel by RV often describe it as the most time they've ever spent together, with the world as a classroom.

## The cons

### 1. Maintenance never stops
RVs combine a house and a vehicle, and both need repairs. Roof seals, slide-outs, plumbing, tires and engines all need attention, and repair shops can have long waits.

### 2. Tight quarters
Small spaces test any relationship. Rainy days stuck inside can feel long. Consider personal space in your floorplan.

### 3. Depreciation
Most RVs lose value quickly, unlike a house that might appreciate.

### 4. Logistics: mail, residency and healthcare
You need a legal domicile for taxes, voter registration and vehicle registration. Health insurance across state lines can be complicated.

### 5. Finding sites
Popular campgrounds book up months ahead, especially in summer and around holidays. You'll spend time planning where to sleep next.

### 6. Internet and power
Remote work requires reliable connectivity, which can be patchy. Off-grid camping needs solar, batteries or a generator.

### 7. Driving and parking a big rig
Large RVs need careful route planning (low bridges, steep grades) and can be stressful to drive and park.

## Must-have gear for RV life

- Surge protector for campground power hookups
- Water pressure regulator and a drinking-water hose
- Leveling blocks and wheel chocks
- Sewer hose kit and gloves
- Portable fire pit where open fires are restricted, like the BioLite FirePit
- Outdoor grill, folding table and comfortable camp chairs
- Lantern and plenty of lighting

## How to try it before committing

1. **Rent first.** Peer-to-peer rental platforms and dealers rent every kind of RV. Try a week, then a month.
2. **Do a long trip before selling the house.** Give yourself a full season before making a permanent decision.
3. **Track every expense** on your trial trip to get a realistic budget.
