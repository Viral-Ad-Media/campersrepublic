Cooking is half the fun of camping, and the stove decides what's on the menu. A backpacker boiling water for a freeze-dried meal needs something completely different from a family making pancakes for six. Here's how the main types compare.

## Camping stove types

| Type | Fuel | Best for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-burner tabletop | Propane (1 lb canisters or bulk tank) | Car camping, groups | Size and weight |
| Single-burner butane | Butane canisters | Small groups, picnics | Weak in the cold |
| High-output freestanding | Propane | Big groups, large pots | Bulk |
| Backpacking canister | Isobutane-propane | Hiking, solo trips | Small pots only |
| Integrated system | Isobutane-propane | Fast boiling, windy spots | Mostly a boiler, not a skillet |
| Wood-burning | Twigs and sticks | Ultralight, emergency backup | Fire bans |
| Solar cooker | Sunshine | Sunny base camps | Slow and weather-dependent |

## Two-burner propane stoves

This is the classic car-camping stove. Coleman's tabletop stoves are everywhere, and Camp Chef (Everest and Explorer), GSI Outdoors and Jetboil (Genesis) make popular higher-end versions.

**What to look for**

- **Burner output and simmer control.** High output boils water fast, but simmering without scorching matters more for real cooking.
- **Wind protection.** Side wind panels make a big difference outdoors.
- **Burner spacing.** Can you fit a 10-inch pan and a pot at the same time?
- **Ignition.** Push-button ignition is convenient, but keep a lighter as a backup.

An adapter hose lets you run most two-burner stoves from a refillable 20 lb tank, which is cheaper and reduces disposable canister waste.

## Single-burner butane stoves

These are compact "suitcase" stoves like those from Gas One and Eureka. They're affordable and simmer well, but butane struggles below about 40°F (4°C), so they're better for summer.

## Freestanding and high-output stoves

Camp Chef Explorer-style stoves stand on legs and can handle stockpots and large griddles. They're great for group camps, hunting camps and tailgates.

## Backpacking canister stoves

Tiny stoves that screw onto an isobutane-propane canister, from brands like MSR, Jetboil, Snow Peak and Kovea. They boil water quickly, weigh very little and fit inside your pot.

## Integrated systems

Jetboil-style stoves combine a burner, a heat-exchanger pot and a windscreen. They're very fuel-efficient for boiling water, but less suited to frying or simmering.

## Wood-burning stoves

Stoves like the Solo Stove Campfire burn twigs and use a double wall to create a cleaner, hotter fire. You never run out of fuel in a forest, but they're often prohibited during fire restrictions. Always check local rules.

## Solar cookers

Solar ovens like the GoSun Cooker use reflectors to cook food slowly with no fuel at all. They're fun and efficient for a sunny base camp, but not something to rely on for a hot dinner after a cloudy day.

## How to choose

1. **Count your campers.** For one or two people a single burner works; for three or more, go for two burners.
2. **Decide how you'll cook.** Mostly boiling water? An integrated system. Real meals? A two-burner with good simmer control.
3. **Think about the temperature.** Propane works in the cold, while butane does not.
4. **Consider weight.** If you carry your kitchen on your back, go for a canister stove.

## Stove safety

- Cook outside the tent. Stoves produce carbon monoxide.
- Set the stove on a stable, level surface away from tent walls and dry grass.
- Check hose and canister connections for leaks with soapy water.
- Let canisters cool before packing, and recycle empties where your local program accepts them.
